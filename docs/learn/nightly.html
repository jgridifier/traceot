3. The practical job (refresher)

Two periods, A and B. Each is a table of units (rows), features (columns), and one outcome whose distribution moved. TRACE’s job is a readable budget of why:

  • Mix (composition): the kinds of units showing up changed.
  • Response (structure): the same kinds of units now produce different outcomes.
  • Entry / exit: units that arrived or left, kept on their own line when IDs are known.
  • Noise: bands and a no-shift test say whether any of the above is bigger than sampling wobble.

Each piece is further split into channels (location, spread, skew, tails) and per feature.

4. Worked vignette: the slower race that wasn’t

Illustrative only: no TRACE run behind this story, and no numbers.

A city 10K’s median finishing time got slower from one year to the next. Two things happened at once: the race drew many more first-time runners (a mix change), and race day was warmer, which slows everyone a little (a response change). Runners who raced both years can be matched by bib history, so the newcomers and the dropouts sit on their own entry / exit lines.

A good budget should say most of the slowdown in the middle of the distribution came from newcomers, while the heat showed up as a small, fairly even shift across everyone, plus extra spread in the slow tail. The averaging trap from last night applies directly: if the method averages each group’s quantile curve and then splits the difference, it can blur “more slow runners” into “everyone got slower,” and the report comes back as a tidy half-and-half. The mixture counterfactual asks the right question instead: what would this year’s finish-time distribution look like with last year’s field and this year’s weather? It answers that by averaging the groups’ CDFs (the share of runners finished by each time), then inverting.

Practical rule: if the explanation would change depending on whether you average curves or average shares-finished-by-time, the mix story isn’t settled yet.

5. How to read a TRACE output tomorrow

  • Budget exactness is bookkeeping health (pieces sum to the model’s W₂²). It still passes. It says nothing about accuracy.
  • Role leakage. On skew_struct (almost pure response change) v4 put comparable mass on mix and response. On skew_comp (almost pure mix) it put most mass on response. Treat the mix/response ratio on shape channels as unreliable today.
  • Feature ranks remain coarse-only: prefer “these two features dominate” over a fine ordering.
  • Bands are still unscored for v4 (coverage INCOMPLETE). v3’s pooled coverage was 0.815 against a 0.90 bar, so intervals stay provisional.
  • Where TRACE is least wrong: response-driven or small-mean-shift cases, consistent with v3 and the v4 tail result. That is a relative statement, not a best-in-class claim.

7. One paper / idea for tonight

Chernozhukov, Fernández-Val & Melly (2013). Inference on Counterfactual Distributions. Econometrica 81(6), 2205–2268. arXiv:0904.0951.

This is the paper behind the CFM rival that keeps beating TRACE on the composition cells, so it is worth reading on its own terms. Its central object is exactly the V5 primitive: a counterfactual distribution built by integrating one period’s conditional CDF over the other period’s covariate mix, then inverting to get quantiles. It also gives functional central limit results and bootstrap bands for those counterfactual curves, which is the part TRACE’s coverage claims have been failing.

When you skim it, focus on the definition of the counterfactual distribution and the section on uniform inference bands. The question for V5 is narrow: if TRACE adopts this object, can its order-free per-feature shares sit on top without losing CFM’s band guarantees?
