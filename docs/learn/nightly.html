3. The practical job (refresher)

Two periods, A and B. Each is a table of units (rows), features (columns), and one outcome whose distribution moved. TRACE’s job is a readable budget of why:

  • Mix (composition): the kinds of units showing up changed.
  • Response (structure): the same kinds of units now produce different outcomes.
  • Entry / exit: units that arrived or left, kept on their own line when IDs are known.
  • Noise: bands and a no-shift test say whether any of the above is bigger than sampling wobble.

Each piece is further split into channels (location, spread, skew, tails) and per feature.

4. Worked vignette: the longer library wait

Illustrative only: no TRACE run behind this story, and no numbers.

A public library notices the typical wait for a book on hold got longer this autumn, and the long tail (people waiting many weeks) got much fatter. Two things changed at once. Patrons placed a bigger share of holds on brand-new popular titles, which always have long queues (a mix change). And the library bought fewer copies per new title, so even the same kind of hold now waits longer (a response change). Patrons who joined or left during the season sit on their own entry / exit lines because card numbers persist.

The trap is the fat tail. If you average each title type’s wait-time curve and compare, the new-title share mostly shows up as a gentle move in the middle, and the fatter tail gets pinned on “fewer copies.” The mixture counterfactual asks the cleaner question: with this autumn’s copy-buying but last autumn’s mix of holds, what share of patrons would be served within each number of weeks? Averaging those shares across title types, then reading off the weeks, puts the tail where it belongs. That is exactly the V5 object F[s,t].

Practical rule: when the tail moved much more than the middle, ask the report for both the curve-average and the shares-served view. If they disagree about mix versus response, trust neither until the first gate is scored.

5. How to read a TRACE output tomorrow

  • Budget exactness is bookkeeping health (pieces sum to the model’s W₂²). It still passes. It says nothing about accuracy.
  • Role leakage. On skew_struct (almost pure response change) v4 put comparable mass on mix and response. On skew_comp (almost pure mix) it put most mass on response. Treat the mix/response ratio on shape channels as unreliable today.
  • Feature ranks remain coarse-only: prefer “these two features dominate” over a fine ordering.
  • Bands are still unscored for v4 (coverage INCOMPLETE). v3’s pooled coverage was 0.815 against a 0.90 bar, so intervals stay provisional.
  • Where TRACE is least wrong: response-driven or small-mean-shift cases, consistent with v3 and the v4 tail result. That is a relative statement, not a best-in-class claim.

7. One paper / idea for tonight

DiNardo, Fortin & Lemieux (1996). Labor Market Institutions and the Distribution of Wages, 1973–1992: A Semiparametric Approach. Econometrica 64(5), 1001–1044. doi:10.2307/2171954.

This is the source of the DFL rival. Its trick reaches the same mixture counterfactual as V5 without ever modelling conditional distributions: keep period B’s rows, but reweight each one by ψ(x) = dFA(x) / dFB(x), estimated with a simple classifier of “which period is this row from?” The reweighted outcome distribution is F[A,B]: period A’s mix, period B’s response. Ignore the specific application and read it for the reweighting identity and the kernel-density pictures of counterfactual distributions.

The practical use for V5: on the first-gate cells, TRACE’s grid-averaged F[s,t] and DFL’s reweighted CDF estimate the same object two different ways. If they disagree badly on the tails, that’s a bug or an overlap problem to fix before scoring, not a result.
