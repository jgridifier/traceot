3. The practical job (refresher)

Two periods, A and B. Each is a table of units (rows), features (columns), and one outcome whose distribution moved. TRACE’s job is a readable budget of why:

  • Mix (composition): the kinds of units showing up changed.
  • Response (structure): the same kinds of units now produce different outcomes.
  • Entry / exit: units that arrived or left, kept on their own line when IDs are known.
  • Noise: bands and a no-shift test say whether any of the above is bigger than sampling wobble.

V5 builds the split from four hybrid distributions: each period’s inputs paired with each period’s conditional outcome model. Those hybrids are only as good as the conditional model is where the other period’s inputs land.

4. Worked vignette: the hotter, newer 10K

Illustrative only: no TRACE run behind this story, and no numbers.

A community 10K compares finish times between two years. This year the field has many more first-timers, including a big group much older than anyone who ran last year (a mix change). Race day was also a few degrees warmer, slowing everyone a bit (a response change). Runners who appear in only one year sit on their own entry / exit lines because bib registrations link across years.

To ask “this year’s field under last year’s conditions,” TRACE needs last year’s model of finish time given age and experience, evaluated on this year’s runners. If that model was a set of bins fitted only on last year’s ages, it simply repeats its oldest bin’s prediction for every older newcomer. The slower times those runners bring then look like something the warm day did, so composition gets booked as response. That is the pattern the diagnosis found: a near-equal and opposite transfer from mix to response.

Practical rule: before reading the mix/response ratio, compare the input ranges of the two periods. If the new period reaches outside the old one, the report’s answer depends on how the mean model extrapolates, and that should be checked, not assumed.

5. How to read a TRACE output tomorrow

  • Budget exactness is bookkeeping health (pieces sum to the total). It still passes. It says nothing about accuracy.
  • Grid and inversion error is negligible: about 10⁻⁶ in the diagnosis, three orders of magnitude below the tolerance. If V5 is wrong, look upstream at the conditional model.
  • Mean model first. In the diagnosis, the mean model accounted for most of v5’s error (0.01807 of 0.02699 on skew_comp, 0.00475 of 0.00971 on tail_comp). Residual-law sampling was a small piece.
  • Heteroskedasticity is not today’s worry. The diagnosis found essentially zero rank correlation between residual size and fitted mean, so a fancier location-scale model has nothing to fix on these cells.
  • Exploratory isn’t a result. The ordinary least squares variant beat the rivals on the spent seeds, but it was chosen after seeing the failure, and the simulator’s mean is linear by construction. Treat it as a reason to run V5.1, not as evidence TRACE works.

7. One paper / idea for tonight

Shimodaira, H. (2000). Improving predictive inference under covariate shift by weighting the log-likelihood function. Journal of Statistical Planning and Inference 90(2), 227–244. doi:10.1016/S0378-3758(00)00115-4.

This paper coined “covariate shift”: the input distribution moves while the conditional law stays put. Its key point is that a correctly specified model is safe under that shift, but a misspecified one is fitted where the old inputs were dense and can be badly wrong where the new inputs land. The fix it studies is importance weighting, which re-centres the fit on the target inputs. That is exactly the V5 failure in plain terms: the binned mean model was misspecified for the covariate tails of the other period.

The practical use for V5.1: ordinary least squares passes only because the simulator’s mean is linear, which is Shimodaira’s “correctly specified” case. The honest follow-up after a pass is a non-linear-mean scenario, where a weighting-style fix of this kind becomes the natural rival to test.
