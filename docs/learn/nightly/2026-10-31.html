3. The practical job (refresher)

Two periods, A and B. Each is a table of units (rows), features (columns), and one outcome whose distribution moved. TRACE’s job is a readable budget of why:

  • Mix (composition): the kinds of units showing up changed.
  • Response (structure): the same kinds of units now produce different outcomes.
  • Entry / exit: units that arrived or left, kept on their own line when IDs are known.
  • Noise: bands and a no-shift test say whether any of the above is bigger than sampling wobble.

V5 builds the split from hybrid distributions: one period’s inputs pushed through the other period’s conditional outcome model. Where one period has inputs the other never saw, that hybrid is an extrapolation, not a measurement.

4. Worked vignette: two summers of tomato plots

Illustrative only: no TRACE run behind this story, and no numbers.

A community garden compares tomato yield per plot across two summers. The second summer, the garden opened a new sunny strip, so some plots got far more daily sun than any plot had the year before (a mix change). The second summer was also drier overall (a response change). Plots worked by the same gardeners both years can be linked; new and abandoned plots go on the entry / exit lines.

The question “second-summer plots under first-summer conditions” asks the first summer’s model what a very sunny plot would have yielded. The first summer has no very sunny plots, so the answer comes entirely from how that model extends past its data. A flat extension says extra sun does nothing; a straight line says it helps a lot. The two choices give very different mix/response budgets from the same gardens.

Practical rule: split the report in two mentally. For units whose inputs sit inside both periods’ range, the mix/response split is data-driven. For units outside the overlap, the split reflects a modelling assumption, and it should be shown as a separate, clearly labelled share rather than blended in.

5. How to read a TRACE output tomorrow

  • Budget exactness is bookkeeping health (pieces sum to the total). It still passes. It says nothing about accuracy.
  • Look for the overlap share. Ask what fraction of each period’s units fall outside the other period’s input range. If it is large, the mix/response ratio is mostly the mean model’s extrapolation. (TRACE does not yet print this; see the build note below.)
  • Mean model first. Yesterday’s diagnosis put most of v5’s error on the mean model (0.01807 of 0.02699 on skew_comp, 0.00475 of 0.00971 on tail_comp). Nothing new today changes that.
  • Exploratory isn’t a result. The least-squares variant looked good on spent seeds, but the simulator’s mean is linear by construction. Only the V5.1 gate on fresh seeds can move the scoreboard.

7. One paper / idea for tonight

Crump, R. K., Hotz, V. J., Imbens, G. W., & Mitnik, O. A. (2009). Dealing with limited overlap in estimation of average treatment effects. Biometrika 96(1), 187–199. doi:10.1093/biomet/asn055.

The setting is two groups whose covariates only partly overlap. Where one group has units the other lacks, any comparison leans on extrapolation and the estimate’s variance blows up. Their practical answer is to estimate on a trimmed region where both groups are well represented, and they give a simple rule for choosing it from the estimated probability of belonging to one group.

Mapped onto TRACE, the two groups are the two periods. A period-membership score per unit would tell a reader which share of the budget sits in well-overlapped territory and which share is extrapolated, which is the vignette’s practical rule in formal terms. It is a candidate diagnostic for after V5.1, not part of the frozen gate.
