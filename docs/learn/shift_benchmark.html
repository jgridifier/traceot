ShiftBench: how well do methods explain a distribution shift?

When an outcome's distribution moves between two periods, you want to know why. This page explains the question, how we built a test with known answers, and how five standard methods did. TRACE joins the same test when its shift decomposition lands.

The question

Compare an outcome in two periods, A and B. Part of the change happens because the mix of units changed: their features moved. That part is called composition. The rest happens because the same features now lead to different outcomes. That part is called structure.

To separate them, imagine a third world: period-B features, but period-A rules. Going from A to that world is composition. Going from that world to B is structure.

The split can be asked of any summary of the outcome: its mean, its spread, or its tails (here the 10th and 90th percentiles). Many methods only answer it for the mean.

A test with known answers

On real data nobody knows the true split, so a method can't be graded. ShiftBench generates data where we planted the split, and computes the true values from two million draws. Its eleven scenarios each stress one thing: no shift at all, features moving, rules changing, both at once, spread changes, tail-only changes with the mean held fixed, correlated features, a feature shift cancelled by a rule change, noise that depends on a feature, curved effects, and units entering the panel. Most scenarios also add inert features that do nothing, to catch methods that blame the wrong feature.

Everything (scenarios, sizes, seeds, metrics, and our predictions about the rivals) was written down and committed before any method ran. That record is the pre-registration.

The five rivals

  • Oaxaca-Blinder (OB): fit a straight-line model in each period and split the mean change. The textbook baseline.
  • DiNardo-Fortin-Lemieux (DFL): reweight period-A units so their features look like period B, then read off any summary of the reweighted outcomes.
  • Chernozhukov-Fernandez-Val-Melly (CFM): model the whole conditional distribution in period A, then average it over period-B features.
  • Firpo-Fortin-Lemieux RIF regression (RIF): turn each summary into a per-unit quantity whose average is that summary, then split it like Oaxaca-Blinder.
  • Shapley drift (SHAP): fit a boosted-tree model per period and split the mean change with Shapley values. A strong, popular choice.

Where TRACE fits

TRACE plugs in through one adapter. It runs on the same frozen data, seeds and metrics. A claim that it beats a rival needs a paired 95% interval that excludes zero at every size.
